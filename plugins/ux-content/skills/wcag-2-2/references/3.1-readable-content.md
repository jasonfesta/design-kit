# 3.1 Readable Content

Covered criteria: 3.1.1 Language of Page (A), 3.1.2 Language of Parts (AA), 3.1.3 Unusual Words (AAA), 3.1.4 Abbreviations (AAA), 3.1.5 Reading Level (AAA), and 3.1.6 Pronunciation (AAA).

Use this for product copy, help, policies, education, healthcare, finance, multilingual content, legal content, onboarding, error messages, and terminology-heavy interfaces.

## Language of Page

Set the default human language of each page or app shell programmatically.

For HTML, this means a correct `lang` value on the root element. In SPAs, ensure the language remains correct after route changes, locale changes, or server/client hydration.

Check generated error pages, embedded app shells, modal-only flows, and authentication pages.

## Language of Parts

Mark passages or phrases whose language differs from the page language, except proper names, technical terms, words of indeterminate language, and words absorbed into the surrounding language.

Apply to:

- Quotes.
- Product descriptions in another language.
- Foreign-language navigation labels.
- Multilingual support content.
- Legal or academic citations where pronunciation matters.

Correct language markup helps screen readers pronounce content and helps translation tools interpret it.

## Unusual Words and Jargon

At AAA, provide definitions for words or phrases used in an unusual or restricted way, including idioms and jargon.

Mechanisms include:

- Inline definition on first use.
- Glossary with clear links.
- Expandable explanation.
- Plain-language summary.
- Contextual help.

Prioritize terms that affect decisions, consent, legal obligations, payments, health, safety, eligibility, or task completion.

## Abbreviations

At AAA, provide the expanded form or meaning of abbreviations.

Rules:

- Expand on first use when space allows.
- Keep the expanded form near the abbreviation for critical content.
- Avoid using an abbreviation in button or link text unless the audience reliably understands it.
- Do not rely only on hover title attributes; they are not consistently available.
- When an abbreviation has multiple meanings, define the intended one.

## Reading Level

At AAA, when text requires reading ability above lower secondary education after removing proper names and titles, provide supplemental content or a simpler version.

Useful supplemental content:

- Plain-language summary.
- Step-by-step instructions.
- Examples.
- Diagrams with accessible alternatives.
- Definitions.
- Audio or video explanation with alternatives.
- Easy-read version for critical public information.

Do not oversimplify legally required wording in a way that changes meaning. Pair formal text with a plain-language explanation.

## Pronunciation

At AAA, provide pronunciation where meaning is ambiguous without it.

Use for:

- Homographs where pronunciation changes meaning.
- Names or technical terms necessary for comprehension.
- Educational content where pronunciation is part of the learning objective.
- Voice interfaces where users must speak the term.

Mechanisms include phonetic spelling, audio pronunciation, pronunciation key, or glossary entry.

## Plain-Language Practice

Even when AAA is not the target, readable content reduces errors and support burden.

- Put the user task first.
- Use common words where precise enough.
- Prefer active voice for instructions.
- Keep sentences focused.
- Use headings that describe actions or topics.
- Put conditions before consequences when users need to decide.
- Avoid idioms, jokes, or cultural references in critical instructions.
- Explain consequences before destructive or financial actions.

## Inclusive Language

Use language that preserves dignity and avoids treating disability as a defect or joke.

- Prefer person-first or identity-first wording according to community or user preference.
- Do not describe people as bound, confined, suffering, normal, special, or defective.
- Avoid metaphors that use disability as a negative concept.
- Name accessibility features directly: captions, keyboard access, screen reader support, accessible parking.

## Acceptance Tests

- Verify page and passage language with browser or accessibility inspection.
- Read all critical paths aloud; unclear wording usually becomes obvious.
- Ask whether a first-time user can identify the next action and consequence.
- List jargon and abbreviations; confirm definitions exist where required.
- Check translation/localization does not remove language markers or definitions.
- Test content with representative users where stakes or complexity are high.

## Common Failures

- Missing root language on an app shell.
- Language changes not marked in multilingual articles.
- Error messages use internal system terms.
- Abbreviations appear in headings or buttons with no expansion.
- Legal text is presented without a plain-language summary.
- Help content explains concepts after the user needed them.
- Pronunciation ambiguity affects meaning in educational or voice-driven content.
